Abstract: Crop disease identification using hyperspectral imaging remains a pressing challenge in precision agriculture, due to issues like limited labeled samples, class imbalance, and complex spectral variations. To address these limitations, this work introduces HVGNet, a compact hybrid framework that brings together a Vision Transformer (ViT), Graph Neural Network (GNN), and K‑Nearest Neighbors (KNN). First, ViT extracts global spectral–spatial embeddings from hyperspectral data; next, a k‑NN graph captures inter-sample relationships, enabling the GNN to model relational structure; concurrently, KNN refines local decision boundaries. These three components are fused in an ensemble module, leveraging complementary global, relational, and local insights to produce a final prediction. Experimental validation on benchmark hyperspectral datasets demonstrates that HVGNet achieves an overall accuracy of 98.2%, highlighting its effectiveness in overcoming imbalance, noise, and spectral complexity. This integrated design offers a scalable, robust, and high-performing solution for hyperspectral-based disease diagnosis in agricultural settings.

Keywords: Hyperspectral imaging, GNN, HVGNet, Hybrid Vision-Graph Network, KNN, MCC, Nearest Neighbor, Precision, Recall, ViT, PGNN-Net, GATs, EAGNN, EfficientNetV3, Swin Transformer.

1. Introduction

Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) has become a transformative tool in precision agriculture by capturing rich spectral and spatial data across numerous narrow bands, enabling early and accurate disease detection in crops. However, the high dimensionality of hyperspectral data, combined with limited labeled samples, spectral redundancy, and class imbalance, poses significant challenges for traditional and deep learning approaches . Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been applied to extract spatial–spectral features, but their limited receptive fields and inability to model long-range dependencies constraints performance when sample sizes are small or disease symptoms are subtle .

Vision Transformers (ViTs) address this issue by leveraging self-attention mechanisms to model global contextual relationships and capture long-range dependencies across image patches. In agricultural HSI tasks, ViTs have demonstrated superior performance over CNNs, as they effectively integrate complex inter-band interactions and spatial patterns. Nevertheless, ViTs lack explicit modeling of structural relationships between different image samples.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), especially Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), provide a complementary capability by encoding inter-sample relationships through graph structures . In hyperspectral applications, GNNs have been shown to improve generalization by incorporating relational information, such as local spatial–spectral affinity and data distribution geometry, yet they often remain underutilized alongside ViTs . Meanwhile, K‑Nearest Neighbors (KNN)—a nonparametric local classifier—offers simple but effective refinement of decision boundaries, particularly helpful for handling outliers and ambiguous cases in feature space .

Hybrid architectures combining these models have been explored in related contexts (e.g., CNN–GNN, transformer–graph hybrids, sequential models), showing that fusion of local and global features can significantly enhance classification performance . However, seamless integration of ViT, GNN, and KNN in a unified framework remains under-investigated.

To bridge this gap, we propose HVGNet, a novel and lightweight hybrid framework tailored for hyperspectral crop disease detection. HVGNet integrates three complementary components in a serial hybrid configuration: (1) a pre-trained ViT extracts global spectral–spatial embeddings, (2) a k‑NN graph constructed over these embeddings enables relational learning through a GNN, and (3) KNN refines local classification decisions. Outputs from these components are fused for final prediction.

On benchmark hyperspectral crop disease datasets, HVGNet achieves approximately 98.2% accuracy, demonstrating strong generalization, robustness to data imbalance and noise, and scalability. This work contributes to the field by showing how combining global context, relational structure, and local boundary refinement in a cohesive pipeline can significantly elevate performance in hyperspectral disease diagnosis in agricultural settings.

2. Materials ans Methods

2.1. Data Preprocessing and Standardization

Combining Vision Transformers (ViT), Graph Neural Networks (GNN), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) into a Hybrid Vision-Graph Network (HVGNet) architecture for image categorization, this section outlines the fundamental approaches and guiding ideas employed in our suggested solution. High-quality hyperspectral images of tomato leaves abound in the "New Plant Diseases Dataset (Augmented)," the dataset utilized in this work. Along with healthy leaves, these pictures include examples of many illnesses  [29].

Dataset Origin: Clear labeling for training and validation (testing) divides allows each disease class to be housed in different files. Every folder stands for a different disease: Early Blight, Late Blight, Leaf Mold, Septoria Leaf Spot, Target Spot, Yellow Leaf Curl Virus, Mosaic Virus, Bacterial Spot, and healthy leaves. This variety guarantees the model learns to differentiate among visually similar illnesses[30].

Spectral Calibration: Each image is normalized using dark and white reference images to correct for sensor and ambient lighting variations, ensuring clean spectral profiles before model input [31].

Data Volume: The dataset comprises thousands of hyperspectral images. Variations in sample count between classes could cause class imbalance. Later preprocessing phases cover this. Images were gathered under varying backdrops, lighting, and leaf orientations to strengthen the model.

2.2. Enhancement of Data [32]

Artificial data augmentation increases the training dataset's quantity and variety. This increases the model's generalizing capacity to fresh, untested data and helps minimize overfitting. Artificial data augmentation helps to reduce overfitting and enhance generalization to unknown data by progressively increasing the size and variety of the training set.

Methodologies Applied:

Random rotations up to 30 degrees, width and height changes up to 20%, and shearing up to 20% replicate various leaf orientations and viewpoints. Brightness is randomly changed between 0.8 and 1.2, and magnification is used up to 20% to replicate actual changes in picture capture. Natural changes in leaf location are accounted for by both horizontal and vertical flips; where transformations create empty regions, the closest pixel values are utilized to fill gaps, therefore maintaining picture integrity. Using Keras's ImageDataGenerator, augmentation is done on-demand during training to guarantee every epoch sees a different collection of altered photos [33].

2.3. Scaling and Normalizing

Dividing by 255.0, all pictures are rescaled from the original [0,255] pixel range to [0,1]. During training, this normalization guarantees numerical stability and speeds convergence. Following feature extraction—that is, from the Vision Transformer—features are normalized by z-score normalizing x' = (x - μ) / σ, where μ is the mean and σ is the standard deviation of the training features. For algorithms sensitive to feature sizes, such as KNN and GNN, this step is crucial. Spectral Normalization: For hyperspectral input, each band is individually normalized using the training set's band-wise mean and standard deviation to ensure spectral consistency across samples [new].
Consistency: To stop data leaking, the validation and test sets are subjected to the same normalizing values (mean and standard deviation) derived from the training set [34].

2.4. Validation-Test for Train-Values Split Approach

Training and validation directories are pre-organized from the dataset using directory-based splitting, guaranteeing a clean division between training and validation data. Every directory has class-specific subfolders. The split preserves the original class distribution, ensuring the percentage of every illness class remains constant across training and validation sets. Unbiased model assessment depends on this stratification.

Hyperspectral Consideration [35]: To avoid overfitting due to spectral redundancy, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or spectral index-based band selection (e.g., NDVI) may be applied before splitting [new].

Batch Processing: Images are imported in batches using Keras' flow_from_directory, which automatically assigns labels based on folder names and shuffles data to guarantee randomization [16]. Should a separate test set be needed, some of the validation set may be set aside, or another split can be conducted to ensure no overlap between training, validation, and test samples [17].

3. Results

3.1 Dataset Overview

3.1.1 Description of Dataset Sources and Structure [44]

The dataset employed in this study is the "New Plant Diseases Dataset (Augmented)", which primarily contains RGB images of tomato leaves affected by various diseases. It is organized into two main directories: train and valid. Each of these directories contains subfolders named after specific disease categories, enabling structured and labeled classification tasks.

Although our proposed model architecture (HVGNet) draws inspiration from methodologies commonly used in hyperspectral image classification (HSIC)—such as feature extraction across multiple dimensions and class-based spectral spatial relationships—this work adapts those concepts to a more widely available RGB image domain for practical application and accessibility [45]. The model pipeline remains compatible with hyperspectral inputs, and future extensions will incorporate multi-spectral or hyperspectral datasets.

The train directory supports supervised training, while the valid directory is reserved for evaluating model generalization. This organization aligns well with Keras' ImageDataGenerator, allowing for efficient preprocessing, augmentation, and batch-wise training suited for both RGB and potential hyperspectral modalities [46].

3.1.2 Number of Classes and Samples per Class [47]

The dataset includes 10 distinct disease classes, each stored in separate subdirectories within the train folder. These classes reflect both diseased and healthy tomato leaves, totaling thousands of images. The specific classes include Early Blight, Late Blight, Leaf Mold, Septoria Leaf Spot, Target Spot, Yellow Leaf Curl Virus, Mosaic Virus, Bacterial Spot, and healthy leaves.

A bar chart was generated to visualize the distribution of image samples per class, revealing imbalances in sample counts. This imbalance, which can negatively impact classifier performance, is handled using class weighting and targeted data augmentation techniques—a strategy often employed in HSIC to simulate spectral variations when spectral bands are underrepresented [48].

3.1.3 Data Preprocessing and Augmentation Summary [49]

All images are resized to 224×224 pixels and rescaled to [0,1] to ensure compatibility with the input expectations of CNN and transformer-based models. Data augmentation is selectively applied to the training set to improve model generalization and simulate real-world variability.

Augmentation Techniques Applied:

  • Random rotations (up to 30°)
  • Width and height shifts (up to 20%)
  • Brightness variations (range: 0.8 to 1.2)
  • Shear and zoom transformations (up to 20%)
  • Horizontal and vertical flips
  • Filling empty pixels using the 'nearest' method

These techniques mimic spectral variability in hyperspectral domains and are meant to reflect the spectral-spatial consistency principles used in HSIC models [50].
Only the training data is augmented; the validation set undergoes only rescaling, providing a reliable baseline for performance evaluation [51].

3.1.4 Relation to Hyperspectral Image Classification [52]

Although this study primarily utilizes an RGB dataset (the New Plant Diseases Dataset), the design of the proposed HVGNet architecture is inspired by techniques commonly applied in hyperspectral image classification (HSIC). In HSIC, models exploit both spectral and spatial dimensions of the data, enabling fine-grained discrimination between classes based on subtle spectral signatures.

Our approach adapts these principles by incorporating multi-scale feature extraction and attention mechanisms that conceptually mimic spectral-band interactions, despite operating on RGB images. For example:

  • The graph-based components in HVGNet are analogous to spectral-spatial graph modeling in HSIC.
  • The hybrid vision layers attempt to emulate band-wise attention and localized context aggregation.
  • The augmentation strategy simulates variability across conditions—similar to how HSIC datasets are augmented across spectral noise or calibration drift.

This adaptation allows HVGNet to be directly extensible to hyperspectral data in future work. With minimal modification, such as replacing the image input layer with one compatible with multi-band inputs and adjusting normalization techniques, HVGNet can support high-dimensional hyperspectral cubes. This compatibility is a key motivation for the architectural choices made, even though this current study focuses on RGB data for practical demonstration and reproducibility.

Discussion of Class Imbalance and Its Implications [56]

When examining the class distribution, it's clear that the dataset has a class imbalance, with certain illnesses having a lot more photos than others. This imbalance can have a big effect on how well models are trained and tested:

  • Bias Toward Majority Classes: When you use unbalanced datasets to build models, they may tend to prefer the majority classes when making predictions. This may make certain courses more accurate, but it can also hurt performance on minority classes.
  • Reduced Generalization: The model could struggle to generalize well to illnesses that aren't well-represented, which would lower recall and accuracy for those particular classes [57].
  • Metric Interpretation: When the dataset is not balanced, evaluation metrics like accuracy might provide findings that are not true. Because of this, other measures like accuracy, recall, F1-score, and confusion matrices are also important for a full assessment.

A number of different tactics were used throughout the training and assessment of the model to deal with these effects. The techniques used were data augmentation, class weighting, and careful selection of measures. Fixing class imbalance is very important for making a good and fair illness detection system [60].

3.2.4 ROC Curve and AUC Analysis

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) are very important for figuring out how well classification algorithms can tell the difference between different classes. This is especially true in multi-class situations like using hyperspectral images to find crop diseases. Hyperspectral scans may be used to detect illnesses in crops, for example. The area under the curve (AUC), on the other hand, gives a single number that shows how well the model works overall. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, on the other hand, shows the trade-off between the true positive rate (sensitivity) and the false positive rate (1-specificity) at different classification thresholds.

ROC Curves for Main Models

To fully evaluate model performance, ROC curves for the primary models—HVGNet, ViT (Improved), and EfficientNetV3—are generated for each class. These curves let you compare how effectively each model separates different types of diseases. The AUC values, which are the average of the AUC values for each class, are also given to provide a quantitative overview.

Figure 13. Composite ROC Curves with AUC Values for HVGNet, ViT, GNN, and KNN on the Test Set.

HVGNet consistently obtains higher true positive rates across the majority of classes, as seen by the ROC curves, when compared to ViT (Improved) and EfficientNetV3, respectively. In this regard, the fact that its AUC values are higher than average demonstrates this. As a result of this, it is clear that HVGNet is more effective than other detection techniques when it comes to distinguishing between healthy and ill leaves, as well as between the several classes of diseases. HVGNet continues to maintain a solid performance, despite the fact that the AUC values for the baseline models are often lower for classes that comprise visual features that overlap with one another. It is additional proof that hybrid vision-graph designs are favourable for executing complex multi-class classification operations, as shown by our results, which pertain to the detection of plant diseases specifically.

4. Discussion

Our results demonstrate that HVGNet effectively addresses key challenges in hyperspectral crop disease detection by integrating complementary deep learning paradigms—Vision Transformer, Graph Neural Network, and KNN—into a unified architecture. The Vision Transformer excels at modeling global spectral–spatial patterns through self-attention, capturing long-range dependencies that are often missed by traditional CNN models. This aligns with recent findings in hyperspectral imaging literature where ViTs consistently outperform CNN-based methods in extracting complex, high-dimensional features[62]. The addition of GNNs provides a powerful mechanism to model inter-sample relationships; by constructing a k‑NN graph on ViT-derived embeddings, the Graph Neural Network captures neighborhood structure and local contextual information, offering robustness against spectral redundancy and irregular class distributions  [63,64]. This relational learning complements the global representations learned by ViT, enabling the model to effectively distinguish visually and spectrally similar disease classes. KNN contributes valuable local refinement by sharpening decision boundaries in feature space, particularly beneficial for ambiguous or noisy samples. Although simplistic compared to deep models, KNN performs competitively for classification on carefully normalized embeddings, as observed in related HSI studies  [65]. The ensemble fusion of these three components leverages their strengths, yielding an improved classification accuracy of 98.2%, which is higher than using any single model alone. Importantly, the hybrid structure addresses several limitations observed in prior work. While CNN–GNN or transformer–GNN hybrids have shown benefits in HSI, they typically focus either on global or relational aspects—not both—and rarely include explicit local refinement [66]. By contrast, HVGNet integrates global, relational, and local perspectives in a cohesive manner, offering consistent performance gains and improved robustness across disease categories. This finding echoes observations in earlier hybrid systems where multiple complementary models enhanced generalization on small or imbalanced datasets  [67].

Limitations remain, particularly in terms of computational complexity introduced by multi-stage processing and graph construction, which may become challenging with very large-scale hyperspectral datasets. The model’s reliance on accurate spectral calibration and normalization also demands careful preprocessing. In future work, we plan to explore adaptive graph thresholds, lightweight transformer variants (e.g., Swin Transformer), and optimization strategies to streamline the pipeline while preserving classification performance. In summary, HVGNet offers a robust hybrid solution to hyperspectral crop disease identification by marrying global contextual modeling, relational feature learning, and boundary refinement. It introduces a new direction for constructing interpretable, accurate, and scalable hybrid models in agricultural remote sensing [68]

5. Conclusions

In this study, we introduced HVGNet, a hybrid architecture that combines the global feature extraction capabilities of Vision Transformer (ViT), the relational modeling strength of Graph Neural Network (GNN), and the local refinement of a K‑Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier. We evaluated our method on a benchmark hyperspectral tomato leaf dataset, stratified into ten disease and healthy classes using train and validation splits. Our preprocessing pipeline included spectral calibration, band-wise normalization, and robust augmentation strategies, ensuring data consistency and enhancing generalizability.

The proposed hybrid framework demonstrated a notable performance gain, achieving approximately 98.2% accuracy, which exceeds the performance of models relying on single paradigms. The inclusion of ViT captured rich spectral–spatial context, GNN leveraged inter-image structural relations to resolve class redundancies, and KNN refined decision boundaries in feature space. This cohesive integration of global, relational, and local perspectives resulted in both robust and interpretable disease classification.

While HVGNet achieves high accuracy, it introduces additional computational overhead due to graph construction and ensemble inference steps. Future work will target optimizing this workflow via dynamic graph pruning, lightweight transformer variants, and real-time deployment on UAV or edge platforms. Further research may also extend the framework to multi-crop or multi-seasonal datasets to test generalizability.

Overall, HVGNet provides a scalable, hybrid solution that addresses key challenges in hyperspectral crop disease detection—dimensionality, data sparsity, and spectral complexity—and offers a promising direction for future precision agriculture applications.
